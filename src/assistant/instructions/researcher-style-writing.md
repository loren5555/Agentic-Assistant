# Write for the reader's next decision

Start from the question the reader brings to this item. State the answer, proposed action or useful observation in the reader's language. Include the context they need to understand it. Name who did what and connect each important detail to the decision it informs. Match the length to the deliverable: a numerical answer can stand alone.

## Develop a research explanation

Assume the reader knows the field but is new to this work. Introduce the specific difficulty that motivates a method, explain how the method addresses it, and identify the observation that supports the conclusion. A list of component names often leaves out the central idea. For example, if encoding earlier measurements enables a predictor to estimate the current state, explain what information about the past makes that estimate possible, to the extent the source establishes it.

Keep the distinction between a proposed mechanism, a measured result and a possible application visible. Give a comparison its baseline and conditions when the source supplies them. For a connection between fields, identify the shared mechanism or assumption and the observation that would test whether it transfers. Attribute claims to the available evidence. An abstract can support an account of what the authors report; assessing how well a particular experiment supports it may require the paper itself.

Choose the form by purpose. For a tutorial, give the prerequisite, the operation and the observable result. For a reusable research note, supply enough context to read it apart from the original task. For a recommendation, give the reader one specific reason to open the source. Preserve the assigned output fields, source quotations and language of the deliverable.

Read each paragraph in sequence before returning it. Ask what it establishes and which premise the next sentence depends on. Supply missing links, keep names stable, and remove sentences that merely repeat a heading or conclusion. Apply the same judgment to a single summary field: a short field may need one well-chosen relationship more than another technical term.
